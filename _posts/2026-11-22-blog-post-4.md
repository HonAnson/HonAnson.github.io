The variational autoencoder (VAE) [https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.6114] is perhaps the most important architecture to learn in modern machine learning. It cleverly integrate randomness into models and brings diversity to generative models (see figure 1).

I remember when I learn about VAE, people always talk about *gaussian distributions*. Where the encoder predicts a gaussian distribution within the latent space, and the decoder decodes the sampled latent code to predict another gaussian distribution for the reconstruction. And the math it leads to is beautiful, with a simple L2 loss for reconstruction and a KL loss for regularization.

This makes me wonder, what about other kinds of distribution? Why don't we try other kinds of distribution, such as the uniform distribution? Woundn't it make more sense if we "allocate" the probability more evenly within the latent space with a uniform random distribution?

This weekend, I will try to give the *uniform variational autoencoder* a good go.


### Architecture

Lets consider a uniform VAE with 2D latent space for easier visualisation, and note that one can easily generalise it to N latent dimensions. 

For simplicity, I choose the prior distribution to be a uniform distribution within [-1,1]^2. The uniform VAE's encoder and decoder will predict something similar to bays-VAE, with the centre position $c$ of the distribution (aken to mean $\mu$), and the width $w$ of the distribution (aken to variance $\sigma$). So each latent code will be drawn from a random point in a rectangle.


### Loss function
We deriving the loss function with the standard approach for deriving the loss function for bays-VAE. Consider a sample in the data $x$, we want the model to maximise the evidence $p(x)$ of each data point. Consider the log evidence:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p_{\theta}(x) &= \log \int p_{\theta}(x,z)\,dz \\
&= \log \int p_{\theta}(z)\, p_{\theta}(x \mid z)\,dz
\end{aligned}
$$

Which the integral is intractable. But we can use the standard trick of divide and multiply by the posterior $q(z|x)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p_{\theta}(x) &= \log \int q(z \mid x)\, \frac{p_{\theta}(z)\, p_{\theta}(x \mid z)}{q(z \mid x)}\,dz \\
&= \log \mathbb{E}_{q(z \mid x)}\left[\frac{p_{\theta}(z)\, p_{\theta}(x \mid z)}{q(z \mid x)}\right]
\end{aligned}
$$


Apply Jensen's inequality:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p_{\theta}(x) &= \log \mathbb{E}_{q(z \mid x)}\left[\frac{p_{\theta}(z)\, p_{\theta}(x \mid z)}{q(z \mid x)}\right] \\
&\geq \mathbb{E}_{q(z \mid x)}\left[\log{\frac{p_{\theta}(z)\, p_{\theta}(x \mid z)}{q(z \mid x)}}\right] \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{q(z \mid x)}\left[ \log{p_{\theta}(x|z)}\right] + \mathbb{E}_{q(z \mid x)}\left[ \log{\frac{p_{\theta}(z)}{q(z \mid x)}}\right]
\end{aligned}
$$

Which is the ELBO. We choose $q$ to be learnable in order to tighten ELBO, so $q = q_{\phi}$. So far this is the same as bays-VAE. But we now have to deal with the terms for uniform distributions. 


Consider the first term, we note that:

$$
p_{\theta}(x \mid z) =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{w}, & x \in \left[c_{\theta}(z) - \dfrac{w}{2},\; c_{\theta}(z) + \dfrac{w}{2}\right] \\
0, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
$$
